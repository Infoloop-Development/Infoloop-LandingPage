## Why a smart-looking site can get no visitors

You paid for a website. It looks good. Six months on, the only people who visit are the ones you sent there yourself. That is a common story, and it is almost never a design problem. It is a problem with what the pages were built to do.

Getting found is not a switch somebody flicks once the site is finished. It is a set of decisions made before the first page is drawn: what pages exist, what each one is for, how fast they load, and how plainly they are written. Those decisions cost nothing while the site is still on paper. They cost a rebuild once it is live.

## What Google is actually doing

Strip away the mystery and it is simple. Somebody types a question. Google looks for a page that answers that exact question, on a site it has some reason to trust, that will load before the person gives up waiting.

So there are three jobs. Have a page for the question. Make it obvious that the page answers it. Make it quick. Nearly everything else is detail hanging off those three.

## Decide what each page is for, before anybody draws it

Start with the questions your buyers actually type. "Garage management software." "Attendance software for a factory." "How much does custom software cost." Those are three different questions and they need three different pages, because a page trying to answer four questions answers none of them properly.

So write the list first. The home page. One page for each thing you sell. One page for each kind of customer you sell to. And somewhere for articles that answer the questions people ask before they are ready to buy.

Then link them sensibly, so somebody reading about your attendance software can reach your factory page in one click. Those links are how you tell Google which of your pages matter most. A site where everything hangs off the home page and nothing links to anything else is telling Google nothing at all.

## One page, one question

Give every page one big heading at the top that says exactly what the page is about, in the words a customer would use rather than the words your trade uses. Then smaller headings underneath, in the order somebody would actually want to read them.

That is not decoration. It is how a reader skimming on a phone and a search engine reading the page both work out what is in front of them. Most sites get it wrong, usually because the headings were chosen to look right in the design rather than to say anything. It costs nothing to fix and it is one of the few things entirely within your control.

## Speed is not a technical detail

A slow page loses twice. Google pushes it down the results, and the people who do reach it leave before it has finished loading. On a phone, on a weak signal, standing in a car park, three seconds feels like a minute.

Speed is mostly restraint. Pictures saved at the size they are shown rather than straight off a camera. No tracking scripts whose reports nobody reads. No video playing behind the headline because it looked good in the mock-up. Webflow will happily let you build a fast site or a slow one; the difference is what you decide to put on the page.

It shows up in money as well as position. We rebuilt an online shop selling direct to the public and its conversion went up 38%, meaning more of the people already visiting actually bought something. Quicker, clearer pages earn more from traffic you have already paid for.

## Spell out the plain facts underneath

Under every page you can put a small block of hidden text that search engines read directly. It states the plain facts and nothing else: this is a company, this is what it does, this is where it is, this is a common question and this is the answer.

It is not a trick and it is not a loophole. It is the difference between hoping Google works something out and simply telling it. Done properly, this is what gets your opening hours, your ratings or your list of common questions shown right there on the results page, taking up more room than the company sitting above you.

## Write the way you would explain it on the phone

The old advice was to repeat a phrase until the page reads badly. That stopped working years ago and now does active harm, because what reads your page is trying to judge whether a human being would find it useful.

So answer the question in the first two lines, before any preamble about your journey so far. Use the words your customers use. Be specific, because a price range beats "competitive pricing" and one real example beats "trusted by many". And put one clear next step at the bottom of every page, since a page that ranks and then leaves people wondering what to do has still failed.

## What it costs to leave it until later

The bill for skipping all this arrives eventually and it is bigger. Pages get rewritten. The site gets restructured. Addresses people have already shared stop working unless somebody carefully points the old ones at the new. And every quiet month in between is a month you are paying somewhere else for visitors you should have been getting for nothing.

Building it in at the start adds almost nothing to the price, because most of it is a matter of what you decide rather than what you buy.

## In short

Decide what each page is for. Say it plainly at the top. Keep the page quick. State the facts underneath in a form search engines can read. Write for the person, not the machine. Do that from the first page and you launch with search already working instead of paying to bolt it on a year later. That is how we build every site, and we carry on running it afterwards rather than handing over a folder and going quiet.
